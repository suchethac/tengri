# The 142.6 s attributed to XLA is mostly warmup, and the tree-depth fix missed a third entry point

**Date:** 2026-08-31
**Verdict:** *(pending — filled from the tables below)*

**Platform:** Linux 6.8 x86_64, Ryzen 9 5900X, **CPU** (`JAX_PLATFORMS=cpu`),
float64, JAX 0.11.0, BlackJAX 1.6.2. CPU rather than the RTX 3060 because
`bench/reports/2026-08-20_cuda_device_matrix.md` measured the CPU ahead by 33x
forward and 13x on the gradient for a *single* galaxy, and every fixture here is
one galaxy. `TENGRI_DISABLE_JAX_CACHE=1` for every compile measurement, so the
compile column is a real XLA compile and not a persistent-cache load.

**The box was shared with three other agents throughout, at load 18-36.** Every
wall clock below is therefore indicative and contended; StableHLO line counts,
R-hat, ESS and divergence counts are deterministic given the seed and are not.
Where a claim rests on a ratio, both rows of the ratio were taken in the same
conditions, and that is said where it matters.

**Precision:** float64 throughout.

**Data / model:** `bench/scripts/benchmark_notebook_sampler.py`'s own fixtures,
unchanged — `05` (nb05 as shipped today, tsnorm SFH, **D = 8**, 14 bands),
`ctl-dpl` (the non-tsnorm control: nb05's bands, mock, seed, SNR and chain count
over a DPL SFH, **D = 8**) and `ctl-jwst` (the D = 9 continuity, 19-band JWST
page — the fixture the 189.4 / 46.8 s numbers were taken on, called `ctl` in
`2026-08-30_mclmc_tuning.md`). Reusing them rather than writing a fixture is
what `tools/check_harness_parity.py` exists for: a compile number measured on a
model no published fit uses explains nothing about the published fit.

**SNR = 20**, `approx="auto"` resolving to `WavePrecomp` with `n_subbands=5`,
i.e. `band_integration="quadrature"`. `WavePrecomp`'s LUT bias is constant in
SNR on the forward model but enters the posterior gradient multiplied by SNR
(~5 % at SNR 30, ~50 % at SNR 300, #1671); at SNR 20 under quadrature no
`PrecompBiasWarning` fired. **No number here may be quoted at a different SNR or
a different `band_integration` without re-measuring.**

## Why this was measured

The brief for this work opened with a ranked list of levers, and the top of it
was compile:

> **Compile is most of a fit** — 189.4 s cold vs 46.8 s warm — 75.3 %, i.e.
> 142.6 s of XLA.

That number is real and is quoted correctly from
`bench/reports/2026-08-30_mclmc_tuning.md`, which took it the obvious way: same
process, same model, same seed, `fit` called twice, cold minus warm. Beside it
sat MCLMC at 10.4 s by the same arithmetic, and the 14x was attributed to NUTS
compiling "a ragged tree-doubling `while` loop with `max_num_doublings=10`"
where MCLMC compiles "a fixed-length scan of one step".

Two things about that are worth checking before anyone spends a week on
compile, and this report checks both.

**First, the attribution.** A `fit` compiles four different things — the forward
model, its gradient, the window adaptation and the sampling scan — and a
whole-fit difference cannot say which. If the tree loop is the cause, the graph
should grow with `max_num_doublings`, and lowering the cap should cut compile.
That is testable directly and nobody had run it.

**Second, and larger: what a warm `fit` actually skips.** `run_nuts` caches its
adaptation on the Model under `_adaptation_cache_key`, which includes a
fingerprint of the target data. A second `fit` on the *same* galaxy therefore
hits that cache and skips `_nuts_warmup_only` **entirely** — not just its
compile, but its execution. So a cold-minus-warm difference taken that way is
not "compile"; it is "compile **plus the whole warmup run**", and those two want
opposite fixes. If the 142.6 s is mostly warmup, then attacking compile is
attacking the smaller half of it.

Everything below follows from separating those.

## Finding 1 — the tree-depth cap does not change the graph at all

`bench/scripts/benchmark_nuts_compile.py` puts each stage of a NUTS fit through
`jax.jit(...).lower(...).compile()` separately, so trace/lower, XLA compile and
the StableHLO line count come out per subgraph rather than lumped into one
cold/warm difference. `--skip-run`, `TENGRI_DISABLE_JAX_CACHE=1`, nb05's own
600 warmup / 600 draw budget, one cell per subprocess:

**nb05 (`05`, D = 8, 14 bands):**

| stage | cap | trace+lower (s) | **XLA compile (s)** | **StableHLO lines** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `logdensity` | — | 0.16 | 0.95 | **2 256** |
| `grad` | — | 0.33 | 1.10 | **3 271** |
| `hmc_warmup` | — | 0.66 | 3.81 | 4 044 |
| `hmc_scan` | — | 0.41 | 2.37 | 3 758 |
| `nuts_warmup` | 2 | 1.23 | 3.65 | **7 883** |
| `nuts_scan` | 2 | 0.68 | 3.71 | **4 396** |
| `nuts_warmup` | 6 | 1.43 | 3.80 | **7 883** |
| `nuts_scan` | 6 | 0.80 | 4.26 | **4 396** |
| `nuts_warmup` | 10 | 1.75 | 6.75 | **7 883** |
| `nuts_scan` | 10 | 1.02 | 6.53 | **4 396** |

**`ctl-dpl` (the non-tsnorm control, same D and bands)** reproduces it to within
two lines everywhere: 2 248 / 3 271 / 4 044 / 3 758, and `nuts_warmup` **7 885**
and `nuts_scan` **4 398** at all three caps.

Three things fall out.

**1. `max_num_doublings` does not enter the graph.** The StableHLO line count is
**byte-identical across caps 2, 6 and 10** — 7 883 every time for the warmup
program and 4 396 every time for the sampling scan, on both fixtures. A
`lax.while_loop` is not unrolled, and BlackJAX's U-turn checkpoint arrays are
sized by a *shape*, not by more operations. So the "ragged tree-doubling `while`
loop with `max_num_doublings=10`" is a real description of NUTS's **runtime**
cost — `2026-08-31_catalog_batched_samplers.md` measured the cap taking a cell
from 54.88 s to 2.14 s — and **not** of its compile cost. Lowering the cap to
buy compile is a lever that does not exist. (Trace+lower does creep with the cap,
1.23 → 1.75 s, which is Python-side tracing of larger checkpoint arrays; XLA
sees the same program.)

The compile column *does* rise with the cap, 3.65 → 3.80 → 6.75 s. That is not
the cap: it is the same program compiling on a box whose load went from 18 to 36
over the session. An identical HLO module cannot cost different amounts of XLA
work, and the identical line count is what says it is identical. This is
Caveat 1 appearing inside a table.

**2. NUTS's graph is 1.6x HMC's, not 14x.** NUTS's two programs are 12 279
StableHLO lines against HMC's 7 802, and NUTS compiles in 7.4-13.3 s against
HMC's 6.2 s depending on when in the session the cell ran. The 14x in
`2026-08-30_mclmc_tuning.md` is not a graph-size ratio.

**3. The forward model is a quarter of it.** The gradient — the program every
sampler pays per leapfrog step — is 3 271 lines and about 1.1 s. NUTS's warmup
program is 7 883, so **the sampler wrapper is roughly three times the size of
the physics it samples**. That is worth knowing before anyone optimizes the
forward model for compile: on this fixture there is 1.1 s there and 12 s in
BlackJAX.

**And the total is the point.** Every compiled program a cold nb05 NUTS fit
needs adds up to **about 13 s of XLA at the deepest cap**, on a fixture of the
same D and band count as the one the brief's 142.6 s came from. That number
cannot be 142.6 s of compile. Finding 2 says what it is.

## Finding 2 — cold minus warm is not compile

Finding 1 says a cold nb05 NUTS fit contains about 13 s of XLA. The brief's
142.6 s came from `ctl-jwst` — D = 9 continuity, 19 JWST bands, 1000 warmup +
400 draws, 2 chains — a bigger model, so some of the gap is fixture. But not an
order of magnitude of it, and the reason is structural rather than about model
size.

`run_nuts` reaches for a cached adaptation before it does anything expensive:

```python
tuning = (int(n_warmup), float(target_accept_rate), int(max_num_doublings))
adapt_key = ("nuts", not use_dense, bool(pathfinder_warmstart), tuning, problem.cache_key)
cached = _get_cached_adaptation(fitter, adapt_key)
...
if cached is not None:
    parameters = cached          # <- _nuts_warmup_only is never called
```

and `_adaptation_cache_key` is `(engine_cache_key, method_key, _data_fingerprint(fitter))`
— **content-keyed on the data**, deliberately, so that "a genuine refit of the
same target still hits". Calling `fit` twice on one galaxy in one process is
exactly a genuine refit of the same target. The second call therefore does not
merely skip compiling the warmup program; it skips **running** it.

So `cold - warm` on that protocol measures `compile + one entire window
adaptation`. Four fits separate them, all in one process so only the first pays
XLA: galaxy A twice (the second is sampling only), then a *different* galaxy B
twice (the first pays a real adaptation against already-compiled programs, the
second does not).

`ctl-jwst`, the fixture the 189.4 / 46.8 s numbers were taken on — D = 9
continuity, 19 JWST bands, 1000 warmup + 400 draws, 2 chains, cap 10, seed 7,
persistent cache **on**, load average **30.5** at settle:

| fit | what it pays | wall (s) |
|---|---|---:|
| `t1` galaxy A, cold | compile + warmup + sampling | **486.87** |
| `t2` galaxy A again | sampling only (adaptation cache hit) | **350.10** |
| `t3` galaxy B, first | warmup + sampling (programs already compiled) | **591.11** |
| `t2_b` galaxy B again | sampling only | **167.71** |

### The estimator this design was built around does not survive its own control

`compile = t1 - t3` came out at **-104.24 s**. A negative compile time is not a
small error, and the reason is in the table: `t2` and `t2_b` are the *same
computation on two galaxies of the same model*, and they differ by **2.09x**
(350.10 against 167.71). Between-galaxy variance is larger than the quantity the
subtraction was trying to isolate, so the estimator has the wrong sign and no
usable magnitude. It is reported here as the reason it was discarded, not as a
measurement — and `warmup_share_of_cold = 0.87` goes with it, since it divides
galaxy B's warmup by galaxy A's cold fit.

**The `t2` / `t2_b` pair earned its place by killing the headline number it was
added to bracket.** Finding 7 predicts exactly this: the adapted step size spans
9.45x across galaxies of one model, so two galaxies are not interchangeable
denominators. Three statements survive, each taken *within one galaxy, inside
one process, under one machine load*, so none of them needs a compile estimate
and none is exposed to the contention Caveat 1 describes.

### 1. Warmup is 2.5x the cost of sampling (galaxy B, within-galaxy)

`t3 - t2_b` = **423.40 s of warmup** against **167.71 s of sampling**, both with
every program already compiled. Warmup is **71.6 % of a zero-compile fit** and
**2.52x** what drawing 400 samples costs. Both terms are galaxy B's own, so this
is the clean statement and it is the one that answers the question.

### 2. Compile *plus* warmup is smaller than the claimed compile alone (galaxy A, within-galaxy)

`t1 - t2` = **136.77 s**, and that is compile **and** warmup together. The brief
claimed **142.6 s of compile**. Finding 1 measured the compile part directly, on
a fixture of comparable D and band count, at **~13 s of XLA** — with the
StableHLO line counts to say the programs are what they are. A bound plus an
independent direct measurement, rather than a subtraction across galaxies: the
142.6 s was overwhelmingly **warmup**.

### 3. The fact that needs no arithmetic

**The run that paid zero compile took 21 % longer than the run that paid full
compile.** `t3` = 591.11 s with every program in hand; `t1` = 486.87 s including
the whole XLA build. Whatever compile costs on this fixture, it is smaller than
the variation between two galaxies of the same model on the same box in the same
minute. That retires "compile is 75.3 % of a fit" more robustly than any number
in this report.

**What would give a sound compile term**, and why it was not run: fit galaxy A
cold, then fit *galaxy A* again in a **fresh process** with the persistent cache
warm and the adaptation cache necessarily cold. That isolates compile without
crossing galaxies. The three statements above already carry the finding, and the
box was at load 30 with the warmup sweep running, so it was left undone rather
than taken badly.

## Finding 3 — the tree-depth cap fix landed on two of the three NUTS entry points

`bench/reports/2026-08-31_catalog_batched_samplers.md` Finding 2 found that
`max_num_doublings` never reached `blackjax.window_adaptation`, so every
"capped" NUTS run adapted at BlackJAX's default of 10 — up to 1023 leapfrogs per
step — on the half where trees are deepest. It fixed `_nuts_full_scan` and
`_nuts_warmup_only`, which serve the single-galaxy path (`run_nuts`) and the
catalog path (`build_catalog_mcmc_engine`), and took one 50 + 50 cell from
**54.88 s to 2.14 s**.

There is a third NUTS entry point and it was missed.
`Fitter._fit_batch_vmap_mcmc` — the `fit_batch(vmap=True)` path — builds its own
adaptation, and on `main` it read:

```python
else:  # mcmc_nuts, mcmc_ghmc, both tune with the NUTS window
    _adapt_algo, _adapt_kwargs = blackjax.nuts, {}
```

while forwarding `max_num_doublings` into the sampling kernel forty lines
further down. That is the same defect in the same shape: the cap applied to the
cheap half only. The HMC arm of the very same `if` does pass
`num_integration_steps` into its adaptation, which is the tell that the omission
was an oversight rather than a policy.

A second, quieter half of it: `_adapt_kernel_key` — the memo key for the
compiled warmup — did not carry `max_num_doublings` either. So two `fit_batch`
calls on one `Fitter` at different caps would have shared one adaptation, and
the second would have sampled at a step size dual-averaged under the first
cap. Finite, plausible, wrong, and silent — the failure mode
`_adaptation_cache_key`'s own docstring is about.

Both are fixed here, and pinned by two tests in
`tests/contract/test_batch_mcmc_adaptation_memo.py` that are **behavioral rather
than source greps** (the distinction #2107 was about): one spies on
`blackjax.window_adaptation` and asserts the cap it was called with, the other
asserts two caps produce two memo entries rather than colliding.

`DEFAULT_MAX_NUM_DOUBLINGS` is 10, BlackJAX's own default, so this changes
nothing for a caller who never asked for a cap — and everything for one who did.

## Finding 4 — AOT already exists here, and it is compiling the wrong shapes

The brief asks whether AOT compilation (`jax.jit(...).lower(...).compile()`)
helps, and whether a compiled artifact can be reused across galaxies or fits.
Both halves already have answers in the tree, and one of them is not the answer
anyone wanted.

**Across galaxies: yes, and it has been true for a long time.**
`_get_flat_logdensity`'s own docstring is the contract — *"the returned
`log_posterior_flat_2arg(position, data_args)` takes `data_args` as a **traced**
JAX argument (not closed over), so the compiled XLA program is reused across
galaxies sharing the same model structure"* — and `tools/bench_compile_time.py`
exists to keep it true, by lowering the loss against two different `data_args`
payloads and asserting no recompile. AOT adds nothing here: `jax.jit` already
caches on avals, so a second galaxy of the same shape never recompiles. The
`data` axis is not where compile is being paid twice.

**Across processes: that is the persistent cache, not AOT.** `.lower().compile()`
does not skip XLA; it just makes the moment explicit. What removes compile from a
*fresh interpreter* is `~/.cache/tengri_jax_cache`, which is Finding 5.

**Across budgets: this is where it goes wrong.** tengri's AOT surface already
exists and is called `prewarm` (`ForwardModel.prewarm` /
`Fitter.prewarm`), documented as *"After this returns, a subsequent `fit` call
with the same `method` skips XLA compilation."* On `main` it implements that as

```python
warmup_kw = {sample_kwarg: 10}
self.run(method=method, key=key, verbose=False, prewarm=False, **warmup_kw)
```

— a throwaway fit at `n_samples=10` and, since nothing else is passed,
`run_nuts`'s default `n_warmup=300` and `n_burnin=100`. But **both NUTS programs
are keyed on the budget**: `_nuts_warmup_only` takes `n_warmup` as a
`static_argnums` entry because it is the `lax.scan` length, and
`_nuts_chain_scan` traces against a `chain_keys` array of shape
`(n_burnin + n_samples, 2)`. A prewarm therefore compiles the pair
`(n_warmup=300, chain=110)`, and a fit at the nb05 shipped budget then asks for
`(n_warmup=600, chain=700)` — two programs it has never seen.

Measured on nb05 at cap 10, `TENGRI_DISABLE_JAX_CACHE=1`, the pre-warm's shapes
against the fit's:

| program | shape | XLA compile (s) | StableHLO lines | load |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `nuts_warmup` | pre-warm's `n_warmup=300` | 6.72 | **7 883** | 27.9 |
| `nuts_warmup` | the fit's `n_warmup=600` | 6.75 | **7 883** | 30-36 |
| `nuts_scan` | pre-warm's `n_burnin+n_samples=110` | 3.57 | **4 396** | 31.6 |
| `nuts_scan` | the fit's `n_burnin+n_samples=700` | 6.53 | **4 396** | 30-36 |

The StableHLO counts are the load-independent column and they are **identical in
both pairs** — 7 883 and 7 883, 4 396 and 4 396. The compile seconds differ
within a pair by up to 1.8x, which is the machine (Caveat 1), not the program.

**The pre-warm's program is exactly the same size and costs exactly the same to
compile as the one the fit needs — and is not it.** Identical StableHLO line
counts say the two modules are structurally the same program; different static
`n_warmup` values say they are different cache entries. So a default
`prewarm(method="mcmc_nuts")` followed by a real fit compiles *two* ~6.7 s
warmup programs where the fit alone would have compiled one.

**`prewarm` therefore makes a cold NUTS fit slower, not faster**, unless the
caller's budget happens to be the default 300 / 10. That is a negative result
about a documented feature: its docstring promises "a subsequent `fit` call with
the same `method` skips XLA compilation", and `method` is not enough — the
budget is part of the key.

**What was changed here, and what deliberately was not.** `Fitter.prewarm` now
takes `**kwargs` and forwards them, so a caller *can* match the fit they are
warming, and the docstring states which knobs are cheap to match
(`max_num_doublings`, `dense_mass_matrix`, `precondition` — they change the
program without changing how much of it runs) and which are not (`n_warmup` /
`n_samples` are the scan lengths, so matching them means the pre-warm does the
fit's own sampling work). The real fix is an AOT path that lowers and compiles
at the caller's shapes *without executing them* — `lower(...).compile()` rather
than a throwaway `run` — and that is not built here, because it means
reproducing `run_nuts`'s MAP-init and preconditioning setup outside `run_nuts`
to get the shapes right, and doing that by copying is how two paths drift apart.
Finding 1 is also why it is not urgent: the whole prize is ~13 s.

## Finding 5 — the cache is at its cap, and it is not the micro-kernels filling it

The brief's hypothesis was that the 8 GiB cap is costing rehits and that
"raising the cap or scoping the key may be nearly free speed". The cap is
genuinely reached — but the natural fix is the wrong one, and the reason is
worth recording because it inverts a plausible guess.

`~/.cache/tengri_jax_cache`, measured on disk with no process running:

| quantity | value |
|---|---:|
| entries (compiled artifacts) | **2 284** |
| total | **8.50 GB** against the 8 GiB (8.59 GB) cap |
| **fraction of the cap** | **0.990** |
| entries under 64 KB | **1 585 (69 %)** |
| ... their share of the bytes | **9.5 MB = 0.11 %** |
| largest single entry | **124.8 MB** |
| top 10 entries | 1.17 GB = **13.8 %** |
| **top 100 entries** | 6.74 GB = **79.2 %** |
| entries whose atime is today (`ls -lu`) | **2 029 of 2 200**, measured minutes earlier |

`min_compile_time_secs = 0.05` is set deliberately so per-filter micro-kernels
persist, and CLAUDE.md records the 141 GB incident (#1507) that the cap answers.
It is natural to read those two facts together and conclude the cap is being
consumed by micro-kernels. **It is not.** Sixty-nine percent of the entries are
under 64 KB and together they hold **9.5 MB — one part in nine hundred** of the
cache; a hundred entries hold four fifths of it. Raising
`min_compile_time_secs` would evict 0.1 % of the bytes and free nothing.

What the cap actually holds is **roughly 75-80 modules of NUTS's size**. The
largest entry here is 124.8 MB and the top ten average 117 MB, which is the scale
of a compiled sampler for a real model. Four worktrees fitting different models
is enough to churn that, and the atime column says exactly that is happening:
92 % of the cache was touched today.

So the honest form of the lever is: **the cap binds on a handful of large
modules, and `TENGRI_JAX_CACHE_MAX_GB` is already the knob.** Nothing in the
eviction policy needs to change for a user who knows their working set is a few
dozen large models; what was missing is the number that tells them so, and
`cache_size_bytes()` cannot supply it: a cache at 8.5 GB looks the same whether
a hundred modules or ten thousand micro-kernels put it there, and only the first
is worth raising a cap for. `tengri.utils.jax_cache.cache_stats()` is added here
and returns exactly the columns above.

## Finding 6 — warmup length against the analytic metric

Warmup is pure overhead once tuned, and the analytic `J^T N^-1 J + I` metric
whitens the posterior to condition **1.000 at the MAP** under full whitening —
`bench/reports/2026-08-31_catalog_preconditioning.md` Finding 3 measured that
exactly, on every galaxy, to the last digit its diagnostic carries. Window
adaptation's job is to discover a step size and a mass matrix; if the metric has
already supplied most of the geometry, there should be less left to discover and
warmup should be shortenable. **Nobody had measured whether it is.**

The `nutswarm` family added to `bench/scripts/benchmark_notebook_sampler.py`
runs the same `mcmc_nuts` at several warmup lengths with and without the metric,
holding draws at the fixture's own shipped count so exactly one thing varies.
The pair at each budget is the experiment: without both arms, "shortening warmup
cost convergence" and "shortening warmup cost convergence *only without the
metric*" produce the same table.

60 is not an arbitrary floor. BlackJAX's window adaptation spends its budget on
three stages — an initial fast window (default 75 steps), a growing sequence of
slow windows that estimate the mass matrix, and a final fast window (default 50)
that re-tunes the step size. At 60 there is essentially no slow window left, so
that row is *step size only, mass matrix barely estimated* — which is precisely
the configuration an analytic metric is supposed to make survivable.

Fixture `ctl-dpl` — the healthy non-tsnorm control, D = 8, 14 bands, 2 chains,
600 draws, one fit per subprocess.

**The warmup-*length* half of this finding is withdrawn** — Finding 9 shows a
seed change moves gradients per draw by 2.06-2.43x, while adjacent warmup
lengths move it 2.05x, so the length axis is entirely inside the noise. What
survives is the *paired* preconditioning comparison at a **fixed** warmup
length, where only one thing differs between the two rows.

| config | wall (s) | **max split-R-hat** | **div** | **min ESS** | s/ESS | grad/draw | worst param |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| `w=60` | 526.5 | 1.0132 | **28** | **200.5** | 2.626 | 121.9 | `dust_tau_diff` |
| `w=60+precond` | 589.1 | 1.0132 | **6** | **119.2** | 4.944 | 118.9 | `sfh_dpl_tau_gyr` |

Seed 7, both rows, `precondition=0.5` the only difference:

* **divergences 28 -> 6, a 4.7x reduction** — the metric is doing exactly what
  `2026-08-31_catalog_preconditioning.md` measured it doing on the catalog path,
  where it took 418 divergences to zero;
* **min ESS 200.5 -> 119.2, a 41 % loss**;
* **max split-R-hat identical to four decimal places**, 1.0132 both ways —
  neither row clears the bar and preconditioning does not move it;
* **gradients per draw unchanged**, 121.9 -> 118.9, so the metric is not
  changing how hard the sampler works, only where it goes;
* net **s/ESS 1.9x worse**.

**So the metric does not rescue a short warmup, and whether that is a bad trade
is not yet decidable.** It buys a **4.7x reduction in the bias diagnostic** for
**41 % of the min ESS**, at unchanged cost. Calling that "no rescue" on the
strength of s/ESS would be exactly the mistake this report has spent its length
warning against: the adoption bar has two clauses, and they are not measuring
the same thing.

* **R-hat and ESS measure efficiency of exploration.** They say how fast chains
  agree and decorrelate.
* **Divergences are the only column that indicates the sampler is exploring the
  *wrong distribution*.** `vi_fullrank` at 1.6 dex with a monotone ELBO and
  `pathfinder` at 0.11x posterior width both had diagnostics that looked fine
  on the first kind and were wrong on the second.

So a 4.7x cut in divergences at unchanged work is **not nothing** — it is
precisely the clause `wcap=5` fails. Whether trading 41 % of the ESS for it is
good depends on whether the divergences were doing any harm, and **that is what
Finding 10's posterior comparison settles.** The judgement is held until it
lands, rather than being settled by seconds per effective sample, which cannot
see the failure in question.

### Preconditioning's value is fixture-dependent, on three independent measurements

This is not a discrepancy to be explained away. Three measurements now agree
that the metric's worth depends on where it is applied:

| measurement | fixture | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| `2026-08-31_catalog_preconditioning.md` | catalog, **D = 3**, 64 galaxies, metric condition 2.4e3-4.2e4 | **dominant** — 418 divergences to zero, 1.67x faster, doubled the converged count |
| `_resolve_whitening_strength`'s docstring | single galaxy, **D = 7** | **loses** — 4 seeds of 4 with the *unpreconditioned* arm converging and the preconditioned one not, at 4-25x worse ESS/s |
| Finding 6, here | `ctl-dpl`, single galaxy, **D = 8** | **mixed** — 4.7x fewer divergences, 41 % less ESS, R-hat unmoved |

The conditions under which it wins are **high posterior conditioning and catalog
scale**, where the analytic metric is near-exact and warmup cannot estimate a
per-galaxy mass matrix from a short window anyway. The conditions under which it
does not are **single-galaxy D = 7-8**, where window adaptation has 600 steps and
one target and can do the job itself. **`ctl-dpl` at D = 8 sits on the losing
side**, and a reader choosing `precondition=` should read the dimension and the
scale, not this report's verdict.

**One paired seed. The repeat is owed**, and Finding 9's seed spread is the
reason that matters: on this fixture a seed change is worth more than most of
the knobs in this report.

## Finding 8 — an unused BlackJAX seam: warmup rediscovers the metric from identity

`blackjax.window_adaptation` in 1.6.2 takes three parameters tengri passes none
of. Verified by reading the installed signature, and by grepping `src/` and
`tests/` for all three, which returns nothing:

```
window_adaptation(algorithm, logdensity_fn, is_mass_matrix_diagonal=True,
                  initial_inverse_mass_matrix=None,   # <- unused
                  imm_shrinkage_to_previous=0.0,      # <- unused
                  initial_step_size=1.0,              # <- unused
                  target_acceptance_rate=0.8, ...)
```

So every NUTS fit in this library starts its adaptation from an **identity**
inverse mass matrix and a step size of 1.0, and spends its warmup budget
rediscovering geometry that `preconditioning.py` computes **analytically** from
`J^T N^-1 J + I`. That is the seam, and it matters for the question this report
was asked: warmup is 2.52x the cost of sampling (Finding 2), and its job is
mostly already done elsewhere.

`imm_shrinkage_to_previous` is the interesting one, because it is not merely a
starting point — it shrinks the *running* estimate toward the supplied matrix,
which is the direct answer to `preconditioning.py`'s own complaint that a dense
mass matrix estimated from warmup draws is noisy and memory-hungry. Shrinkage is
what could make a **short** warmup viable rather than merely cheap: at 60 steps
the slow window barely exists (Finding 6), and an estimator shrunk toward a
correct prior degrades far more gracefully than one built from 60 draws.

**Which arm it would serve, and it is not the obvious one.** Under
`precondition=True` the sampler already works in whitened coordinates where the
target inverse mass matrix is approximately the identity, so seeding it with the
identity is close to a no-op and the seam buys little. The seam matters for the
**unpreconditioned** path: it is how the analytic metric could be delivered to
the sampler *without* a coordinate change, keeping the draws in the original
basis and leaving `problem.restore` out of the picture entirely. That is a
genuinely different design from what `precondition=` does today, not a tuning of
it.

**This is a seam, not a result.** Nothing here measures it — no arm of this
report passes any of the three parameters. It is recorded because it was
verified to be unused and because it is the cheapest untried lever on the
warmup half, and it should be measured with the two-arm design Finding 6 uses,
for the reason that report's own caveat gives: half whitening beat full on 7 of
7 paired comparisons (#1442), so the MAP-exact metric **overshoots** and warmup
is doing some real work. "Warmup is redundant given the metric" is the
hypothesis this seam would test, not a conclusion anything here supports.

it looks. Not measured here.

**One paired seed, and `w=600+precond` seed 8 is running.**

### The combination is the cell this campaign points at, and it was missing

The two effects measured here are complementary in exactly the right way, and
no row tested them together until now.

* The depth cap's **entire liability is divergences**: 20 at seed 7 and 15 at
  seed 8, against the control's 1. Everything else about it is favourable.
* The metric's measured effect on **this same fixture** is **divergences
  28 -> 6 at essentially unchanged gradients per draw** (121.9 -> 118.9,
  Finding 6). It costs almost nothing in work and removes most of the
  divergences.

So the metric is cheap in precisely the currency the cap is expensive in. If it
knocks `wcap=5`'s 20 divergences down by a similar factor while leaving
gradients per draw near 112, **that combination — not either arm alone — is the
deliverable of this campaign**: the cap's speedup with its bias risk bought
back. It is running at seed 7, paired against `wcap=5` alone, and it was
prioritized ahead of `wcap=3` and the remaining warmup-length rows, which sit on
the noise floor Finding 9 characterized.

### Read the divergence column first

`wcap=5` reports **20 divergences against the control's 1**, while showing a
*better* max split-R-hat (1.0017 against 1.0051) and a *better* min ESS (257.3
against 215.3).

That combination is not a clean win, and this project has already been burned
twice by treating it as one. R-hat asks whether chains agree with each other;
ESS asks how fast they decorrelate. **Neither can see a sampler that is
efficiently and consistently exploring the wrong distribution** — which is what
`vi_fullrank` did at 1.6 dex with a monotone ELBO, and what `pathfinder` did at
0.11x posterior width with healthy-looking diagnostics. Divergences are the one
column here that indicates *bias* rather than inefficiency, and the capped run
has twenty times more of them.

### The headline is 6.2x, not 8.9x, and the table says so itself

`wall / grad-per-draw` is a **contention diagnostic obtained for free** from two
columns already published. These fits are gradient-bound, so if time per
gradient were constant that column would be constant. It is not: `wcap=5` sits
at **2.84** and the control at **4.09**, a **1.44x** difference that is the
machine, not the sampler — the control ran through the session's peak load and
`wcap=5` did not.

Decomposing the observed wall ratio accordingly:

| quantity | value | what it is |
|---|---:|---|
| gradient evaluations per draw, control / `wcap=5` | **6.16x** | **real reduction in work** |
| observed wall ratio | 8.86x | what the clock showed |
| residual | 1.44x | the box |

**So the finding is a 6.2x reduction in gradient evaluations per draw at
seed 7**, and 8.9x is quoted only as the wall ratio observed under load 24-34.
The seed qualifier is not decoration: `wcap=5`'s own gradient count moves 2.43x
between seeds (below), so this is one paired measurement, not a characterized
effect. The structural
number is the one a reader on other hardware can reproduce, and it is the
smaller and harder-to-attack claim. This is the same discipline Caveat 1 and the
`>= 8` lower bound applied, turned on this report's own headline.

The `wall/grad` column is **only interpretable within one fixture**: different D
and band counts change the per-gradient cost outright, so the numbers above
compare only to each other.

### The seed spread, in the same contention-immune column

Expressing the seed-8 result in gradients per draw rather than in wall clock
makes the withdrawal of the warmup-length claims arithmetic rather than
judgement:

| comparison | ratio in grad/draw |
|---|---:|
| **`w=60`, seed 7 vs seed 8 — identical configuration** | **2.06x** |
| `w=60` -> `w=100`, adjacent warmup lengths, same seed | **2.05x** |
| `w=60` -> `w=600`, the endpoints, same seed | 5.69x |
| **control -> `wcap=5`, the depth cap** | **6.16x** |

**Changing the seed and changing the warmup length from 60 to 100 move the work
done by the same factor, to within a percent — 2.06x against 2.05x.** So the
adjacent-length comparison is exactly at the noise floor and carries no
information. No claim about warmup length is made anywhere in this report.

**And then `wcap=5`'s own seed-8 repeat arrived and weakened this argument, so
it is restated rather than defended.** `wcap=5` goes **112.7 -> 274.1**
gradients per draw between seeds — a **2.43x** spread on an identical
configuration, slightly *larger* than `w=60`'s 2.06x. So the noise floor applies
to **both arms of the ratio**, not only to the one being compared against, and a
single-seed 6.16x is not "three times a noise floor" in any defensible sense.
That sentence was wrong when written and is withdrawn.

What the two repeats do show is that **seed 8 is a harder target for
everything**: `w=60` rises 2.06x and `wcap=5` rises 2.43x going from seed 7 to
seed 8. A shared difficulty like that largely cancels in a **paired,
within-seed** ratio — which is the only form of this comparison that means
anything, and which currently exists at **one seed only**, because the control
arm was killed before it reached seed 8:

| seed | control grad/draw | `wcap=5` grad/draw | paired ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 7 | 693.8 | 112.7 | **6.16x** |
| 8 | **running** | 274.1 | **unknown** |

**Until that cell lands, the depth-cap effect is one paired measurement.** If
the control at seed 8 rises by roughly the same factor the other rows did, the
ratio holds and the finding stands; if it does not, the 6.16x was substantially
seed 7. The report will not choose between those in advance, and no
recommendation rests on it.

So the speed columns above are **not** the finding. Finding 10 is.

### The mechanism, which is more interesting than the speedup

The `grad/draw` column says what the cap actually does, and it is not what the
name suggests. `wcap=5` pays **112.7 gradients per draw against the control's
693.8** — a 6.2x reduction in the *sampling* half, which the cap does not touch
at all. The cap applies only to warmup.

So the depth cap is not saving time by making warmup cheaper. It is changing
**what warmup concludes**: a capped adaptation cannot complete the deep
trajectories that a small step size implies, so dual averaging settles on a
**larger step size**, and the sampling phase then runs much shallower trees.
That is one mechanism producing every column at once — the 8.9x wall, the 6.2x
fewer gradients, and the twenty divergences. A larger step size is exactly what
buys speed and exactly what causes divergences.

Which means this is the same phenomenon as
`bench/reports/2026-04-22_pathfinder_vs_window_nuts.md`'s **18x regression from
an under-adapted step size**, approached from the other side: there the
under-adapted step size was too *small* and cost time; here it is too *large*
and saves it. Whether the posterior survives is the question, not whether the
clock does.

### Shortening warmup is non-monotonic, which is its own warning

`w=100` is *worse* than `w=60` on R-hat (1.0278 against 1.0132) and on min ESS
(170.3 against 200.5) while costing 1.6x the wall, and it pays 250.2 gradients
per draw against 121.9.

**And the second seed makes that worse, not better.** `w=60` at seed 8 gives
R-hat **1.0031 and 3 divergences** where seed 7 gave **1.0132 and 28** — the
same configuration, one seed apart, moving from "clearly fails the bar" to
"nearly clears it", with the wall going 526.5 -> 915.5 s and gradients per draw
121.9 -> 250.9. That is a larger spread than any difference between the warmup
*lengths* in this table.

So no claim about warmup length is supportable from these rows, and none is
made. The depth-cap comparison survives only because its effect (8.9x, 6.2x
fewer gradients) is far outside that spread — and even there the seed-8
repeat is still owed.

### On the empty-log incident

The control's wall clock, 2 834.9 s, confirms the lower bound this section
carried before it landed (`>= ~2 600 s, still climbing`) and confirms the
methodological point beside it: `.warmup_ctl_dpl.log` was **0 bytes for 47
minutes and then produced its header and row together**, because a redirected
stdout is block-buffered and the whole table fits in one 4 KB buffer. An empty
log was never evidence of "still running". What established that the control had
completed zero cells was the absence of its `--json` file, which
`_append_json` writes and closes per row. The Reproduce section carries the
rule.


## Finding 10 — does the capped warmup sample the same posterior?

*(running — this is the report's central question and its verdict is pending)*

Finding 9's speedup is only real if the posterior is. The measurement is
`bench/scripts/benchmark_warmup_cap_posterior.py`: both configurations on the
same model, mock, seed and MAP warm start, **in one process**, so nothing but
`warmup_max_num_doublings` differs, reporting **per free parameter**

* marginal mean and sd under each arm;
* the **mean shift in units of the control's sd** — the number that says whether
  a scientific conclusion would change;
* the **sd ratio** — the number that catches a collapsed or inflated width,
  which is how `pathfinder` failed at 0.11x while its R-hat looked fine;
* the shift in units of the **joint Monte Carlo error**, computed as
  `sd / sqrt(ESS)` and **not** `sd / sqrt(n_draws)`: autocorrelated draws do not
  supply `n` independent ones, and using the draw count would manufacture
  agreement that has not been demonstrated.

Per parameter, never in aggregate, for #1436's reason — *"coverage has to be
enumerated by seam, not by 'a representative model'"* — an aggregate hides the
one direction that moved. Two candidates are named in advance:
**`sfh_dpl_age_gyr`**, the control's worst parameter, and **`dust_tau_diff`**,
the capped run's.

It also splits divergences by **phase**. `Posterior.diagnostics` carries
`n_divergent` over kept draws and, separately, `warmup_divergence_frac` for the
final warmup window. Divergences while a capped warmup is still adapting mean
something different from divergences during sampling under a step size that
warmup mis-tuned: the first is an adaptation working through a bad region, the
second is the adapted step size being wrong. Finding 9's mechanism predicts the
second.

### The control that decides whether this knob should exist at all

The mechanism above has an alternative explanation, and it is the more likely
one. *"Adapt to a larger step size, run shallower trees, accept more
divergences"* is precisely what **`target_accept_rate`** does, and `run_nuts`
has taken that parameter all along at a default of **0.85**. So the whole 8.9x
may be an **indirect, uncontrolled re-parameterization of a knob that already
ships** — in which case `warmup_max_num_doublings` adds nothing, and the honest
finding is *"nobody had tuned `target_accept_rate`, and it was worth ~9x"*.
That would be a **better** result for a user than a new parameter: no new code,
no new tests, no new failure mode.

The control is the same fixture and seed at the default cap 10 with the target
lowered to **0.65** and **0.6** — 0.651 being ChEES's own default, chosen
deliberately *not* to be NUTS's, and 0.6 the classic NUTS floor. **The top rung
is the shipped default, 0.85**, verified rather than assumed: `ctl-dpl`'s
`shipped` dict sets no `target_accept_rate` at all, so the control arm falls
through to `run_nuts`'s own signature default (`nuts.py:201`,
`target_accept_rate=0.85`). The ladder is therefore **0.85 / 0.65 / 0.6** and
its top rung is the same configuration as the published `w=600` row. That
mattered enough to check: had the top rung been 0.8, it would already have been
a lowered target and the ladder would have carried a silent unmeasured
0.85 -> 0.8 step, confounding any "the cap reproduces a lowered target"
conclusion.

**And 0.85 is not what every path in this codebase uses.** `mcmc_nuts`,
`mcmc_hmc`, the catalog engine and `mcmc_dynamic_hmc` all default to **0.85**;
`mclmc.py:521` uses **0.65**; and `_hierarchical_flat.py:698` runs NUTS at
**0.8**, with `mclmc_target_accept_rate=0.65` beside it. So the hierarchical
path already samples at a different acceptance target from the single-galaxy
path, un-remarked and undocumented. That is cosmetic only for as long as the
target does not matter much — and this ladder is the measurement that decides
whether it does. If lowering 0.85 is worth a large factor, three different
defaults across one library stops being a tidiness question. It is run
through the same instrument, so the discriminating columns come out together:

* **the adapted step size itself** — the quantity the mechanism is about, and
  the only one here that is contention-immune;
* mean tree depth and gradients per draw in the **sampling** phase;
* divergences, **split by phase**;
* wall, s/ESS, `wall / grad-per-draw` as its own contention check, and the
  per-parameter posterior agreement above.

Its table is headed **`target_accept_rate = 0.85 (shipped default) / 0.65 /
0.6`**, with the top rung's value written out rather than implied. That is the
whole point of having checked it: a top rung of 0.8 would already have been a
lowered target, and the ladder would have carried a silent, unmeasured
0.85 -> 0.8 step confounding every comparison in it.

Three outcomes, all publishable:

1. **The lowered-target rows reproduce `wcap=5`'s step size, depth, speed and
   divergence count.** Then the cap is a re-parameterization of
   `target_accept_rate`, Finding 9 becomes *"tune the acceptance target"*, and
   `warmup_max_num_doublings` is justified only if it does something the target
   cannot.
2. **They differ.** Then the cap is doing something genuinely distinct — most
   plausibly bounding *warmup's own* cost rather than only its conclusion — and
   the step-size numbers are the evidence for what.
3. **The lowered target is strictly better** (same speed, fewer divergences).
   Then that is the recommendation and the cap is a curiosity.

**And if a lowered target also shifts the posterior, that is worth knowing on
its own**: it would mean the default 0.85 is load-bearing for *correctness* on
these posteriors and not merely for efficiency, which is a claim about the
shipped default rather than about either experimental knob.

**The two outcomes, written down before the numbers arrive so the conclusion
cannot be fitted to them:**

* **If the marginals agree within MC error on every parameter** — then the depth
  cap buys a genuine ~9x on the dominant cost of a NUTS fit, and it is the
  headline of this report.
* **If they do not** — then the cap buys speed by under-adapting the step size,
  the same mechanism as the 18x regression in
  `2026-04-22_pathfinder_vs_window_nuts.md` arrived at from the other direction,
  and the knob ships **defaulting off** with that stated in its docstring.

Either way `warmup_max_num_doublings` defaults to `None` (equal to the sampling
cap), so nothing changes for a caller who does not ask for it.

## Finding 7 — what per-galaxy adaptation discovers, and what sharing it would cost

Three conventions exist in this codebase and `backends/mcmc/catalog.py`'s module
docstring already names them: per-galaxy adaptation inside the vmap (the catalog
engine), one adaptation on the first galaxy reused for all
(`Fitter._fit_batch_vmap_mcmc`), and adaptation across a chain ensemble (ChEES).
The brief asks what the first buys over the second, since the second is cheaper
by a factor of N in warmup.

There is one piece of evidence already in the tree and it points the *other*
way. `_adaptation_cache_key` includes a fingerprint of the target data, and its
docstring says why:

> Without the data in the key, the ordinary catalog loop that reuses one model
> hands every galaxy the first galaxy's step size and mass matrix [...] This is
> the defect `_data_fingerprint` was written for (issue #1529), where the *MAP*
> cache seeded every fit from the first galaxy's optimum and **killed six of
> eight NUTS fits**.

So the sibling cache has already been measured doing exactly the harmful thing,
on this codebase, on NUTS. That is not proof about the adaptation cache — a MAP
seed and a step size fail differently — but it is the reason the honest
measurement here is a spread, not a wall clock.

`benchmark_catalog_compile.py --adapt-spread` runs the *same* window adaptation
`run_one` runs, on every galaxy of the throughput harness's own `dpl` fixture,
and reports what it comes back with. N = 64, D = 3, 100 warmup steps, cap 10,
diagonal mass, no preconditioning, CUDA:

| quantity | min | median | max | **max / min** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| adapted step size | 0.0274 | 0.0837 | 0.2592 | **9.45x** |
| inverse mass matrix, worst dimension | — | — | — | **5 270x** |

**Per-galaxy adaptation discovers a great deal, and the cheap convention would
throw it away.** Convention 2 hands every galaxy the first galaxy's numbers. On
this fixture that means handing the median galaxy a step size the adaptation
would have put **3x** away from where it landed, and the extreme galaxies one
**9.45x** away — in a sampler where the step size *is* the accept rate. The mass
matrix is worse by orders of magnitude.

This is the opposite of what the cheap convention needs to be true, and it
matches the direction of the metric evidence rather than contradicting it: the
same fixture's per-galaxy metric condition number was measured spanning
**2.4e3 to 4.2e4, an 18x spread** (`2026-08-31_catalog_preconditioning.md`
Finding 1). Sixty-four galaxies of the same model, differing only in their
noise realization and their true parameters, do not share one geometry.

**Two caveats, and the second is the one that could still rescue the cheap
convention.**

1. **D = 3 on the `dpl` fixture.** Every magnitude here is that fixture's. The
   mechanism — `J` is the Jacobian at *that* galaxy's point and `N` is *that*
   galaxy's noise — is structural and D-independent, but the numbers are not.
2. **100 warmup steps is a short window, so part of the spread is estimator
   variance rather than real posterior difference.** BlackJAX's default first
   and last windows are 75 and 50 steps, so at 100 the slow window that
   estimates the mass matrix is very thin — which is precisely why the mass
   column's 5 270x should not be read as 5 270x of *geometry*. The step-size
   column is the more trustworthy of the two, because dual averaging converges
   faster than a covariance estimate. **The control that separates them is to
   re-run this probe at `--warmup 600` and see whether the spread shrinks**; if
   it holds, it is geometry, and if it collapses, most of it was noise. That was
   not run — the 100-step probe alone cost 1 272 s of wall.

**The wall clock is itself a finding, and it is the lock-step penalty in the
open.** One vmapped 100-step window adaptation over 64 lanes at D = 3 took
**1 272 s on the GPU** (load average 23-53 over the window, so contended, and
the ratio to a single lane was not measured). That is ~12.7 s per adaptation
step for a three-parameter model — because every step runs to the deepest tree
any of the 64 lanes asks for, at cap 10, while the step size is still
unconverged. It is the same quantity
`2026-08-31_catalog_batched_samplers.md` Finding 2 measured as 36 s of a 54.88 s
cell, seen from the other side.

**Verdict on the lever: do not build the shared-adaptation engine.** The brief's
hypothesis was "if per-galaxy adaptation buys little, the cheaper convention is
a large win at catalog scale". It buys a lot. The engine change is cheap
(~60 lines) and would indeed remove `(N-1)/N` of the warmup cost, but it would
buy that by sampling most of the catalog at another galaxy's step size, and
`_adaptation_cache_key`'s docstring already records the sibling version of that
mistake killing six of eight NUTS fits.

## FSM-NUTS: scoped, and not built

Dance, Glaser, Orbanz & Adams, *"Efficiently Vectorized MCMC on Modern
Accelerators"*, ICML 2025 Spotlight (PMLR v267;
[arXiv:2503.17405](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2503.17405)) recasts a single-chain
sampler as a finite state machine so that `vmap`ped chains do not stall on the
slowest. It is the named fix for the batched penalty
`bench/reports/2026-08-31_catalog_batched_samplers.md` Finding 1 measured: NUTS
**2.1x slower for 8x the width** while HMC is flat in K.

The brief scoped this as "a vendored reimplementation of the tree builder". That
was too pessimistic, and the correction matters: **the authors released code**,
at [`hwdance/jax-fsm-mcmc`](https://github.com/hwdance/jax-fsm-mcmc), and it
**builds on BlackJAX** rather than replacing it. So the question is adaptation of
licensed, published, interoperating code — the same move
`backends/nested/nss.py` already makes for the nested-sampling branch.

What the code actually says, read rather than assumed:

| question | answer |
|---|---|
| NUTS FSM size | `src/FSM/mcmc/nuts_bundle.py`, **531 lines**, plus `src/FSM/base/run_fsm_in.py`, 248 — **779 total**, and no other in-repo imports |
| dense metric? | **Yes.** `NutsFSM.__init__(step_size, max_num_expansions, divergence_threshold, inverse_mass_matrix, logdensity_fn)` routes straight into `blackjax.mcmc.metrics.default_metric`, which dispatches on rank, so a dense `(D, D)` inverse mass matrix is accepted |
| BlackJAX 1.6.2? | **Every import still resolves.** The repo pins 1.2.4, but `hmc`, `integrators`, `metrics`, `proposal`, `trajectory` and `termination` all still carry the symbols it uses, and the constructor runs against the installed 1.6.2 |
| batches? | State is a `jax_dataclasses` pytree; `step` dispatches five phases through `lax.cond`. No Python branch on a traced value, no `.item()` / `float()`. The README's own example is `jax.vmap(jax.jit(fsm.step))` |
| adaptation? | **None. Anywhere in the repo.** `step_size` and `inverse_mass_matrix` are constructor arguments, assumed already tuned |
| kernel shape | `fsm.step(fsm_idx, state) -> (fsm_idx, state, sample_taken, sample_loc)`, no `rng_key` argument. **Not** the `(rng_key, state) -> (state, info)` shape `blackjax.window_adaptation` drives |
| tests | **None.** No `tests/`, no `pytest`, no numerical check against BlackJAX's own NUTS anywhere in the repo |
| licence | `LICENSE` is Apache 2.0 with the boilerplate **unfilled** (`Copyright [yyyy] [name of copyright owner]`), no per-file headers, and `setup.py` says `license="MIT"` — the repo contradicts itself |
| small-m numbers | none. Every quantitative claim is at **m >= 100**; the README's one figure is ~10x at m = 100, and its worked example uses 128 chains |

Two of those rows decide it, and neither is about effort.

**1. It does not carry adaptation, and warmup is the expensive half here.**
`bench/reports/2026-08-31_catalog_batched_samplers.md` Finding 2 measured **36
of 54.88 s** of a K = 1 catalog NUTS cell inside `window_adaptation` — the half
where the step size has not converged and trees are therefore deepest and most
heterogeneous across lanes, i.e. the half where the lock-step penalty is
*worst*. Finding 2 below finds the same shape on a single-galaxy fit.
`NutsFSM` cannot be driven by `window_adaptation` —
wrong kernel signature — so an FSM-NUTS in tengri would either keep BlackJAX's
ragged `while_loop` NUTS for warmup, leaving the larger half of the lock-step
penalty exactly where it is, or need a second FSM for the adaptation, which the
paper does not provide and the repo does not contain.

**2. The reported win lives at a chain count tengri does not run.** The paper's
mechanism is that the mean leapfrog-loop iterations per draw grows with the
number of lanes — about 6 at 1 chain, ~18 at 1024 — so the benefit is a function
of `m`. `CatalogFitter._run_native_mcmc` runs `lax.map(batch_size=K)` at
K = 8-64, and the measured tengri penalty at K = 8 is 2.1x. An FSM would recover
some fraction of that 2.1x on the sampling half only. Nothing in the paper or
the repo reports m = 8 or m = 64, and the authors' own framing implies the win
shrinks toward nothing there.

**Cost estimate.** 779 lines to vendor, but that is the small part. Around it:
an SPDX/attribution header in the `nss.py` pattern against a `LICENSE` whose
copyright line is blank and a `setup.py` that names a different licence (a
question for a human, not an agent); a numerical-agreement test against
`blackjax.nuts` that the upstream repo does not have and this project's rules
require; a warmup story that does not exist yet; plumbing through
`build_catalog_mcmc_engine`'s preconditioned `run_one`; and `tier="experimental"`
registration with its conformance suite. Realistically **1500-2500 lines
including tests**, with the largest single risk being that after all of it the
measured win at K = 8-64 is small, because that is what the paper's own
mechanism predicts.

**Recommendation: do not build it now.** Not because it is hard, and not because
the code is unusable — it is usable, it accepts the analytic metric, and it
works against the installed BlackJAX. Because the lever it pulls is the smaller
half of the smaller problem on this workload. The condition that would change
that answer is specific and worth writing down: **if a catalog configuration is
found that converges at K >= 128**, the batched penalty becomes the binding
constraint and this is the fix for it. Today no catalog configuration converges
at any K (`2026-08-31_catalog_preconditioning.md`: best 25-38 of 64 at a worst
converged ESS of 1.90 of 200 draws), so the penalty is not what is stopping
anyone.

## What each lever bought

| lever | what it was expected to buy | what it bought |
|---|---|---|
| AOT compilation | cross-galaxy and cross-fit artifact reuse | **nothing new.** Cross-galaxy reuse already holds (`data_args` is traced, pinned by `tools/bench_compile_time.py`); cross-process reuse is the persistent cache; `prewarm` is the AOT surface and Finding 4 says it compiles the wrong shapes |
| raising the cache cap | "nearly free speed" from lost rehits | **the diagnosis was wrong-shaped.** The cap is at 99.0 % but 69 % of entries hold 0.11 % of the bytes — `min_compile_time_secs` is not the culprit and raising it frees nothing. `cache_stats()` added so the question is answerable |
| lowering `max_num_doublings` for compile | smaller graph | **zero.** StableHLO is byte-identical at caps 2, 6 and 10 (Finding 1). It is a runtime knob only |
| `donate_argnums` / shape stability | fewer retraces | **not pursued.** Finding 1 shows compile is ~13 s of a fit, not 142.6 s; the retrace that matters is the budget-shaped one in Finding 4 |
| the tree-depth cap reaching batch warmup | — (not on the brief) | **found and fixed.** A third NUTS entry point still had the #2101 defect (Finding 3) |
| FSM-NUTS | ~10x at 100 chains | **declined, with the code read** — it carries no adaptation, and the win is at m >= 100 where tengri runs K = 8-64 |
| reusing adaptation across galaxies | "if it buys little, the cheaper convention is a large win at catalog scale" | **refuted.** It buys a lot: the step size spans **9.45x** across 64 galaxies of one model (Finding 7). The engine change is ~60 lines and would sample most of a catalog at another galaxy's step size |
| shortening warmup under the analytic metric | less for window adaptation to discover | **no rescue.** Paired at w=60 seed 7: divergences 28 -> 6, but min ESS 200.5 -> 119.2 and max split-R-hat identical at 1.0132. Net s/ESS 1.9x worse (Finding 6) |
| shortening warmup, on its own | warmup is pure overhead once tuned | **unmeasurable here.** A seed change moves gradients/draw 2.06-2.43x; adjacent warmup lengths move it 2.05x. The axis is inside the noise, so no claim is made (Finding 9) |
| capping warmup's *depth* (`warmup_max_num_doublings`, new) | — (not on the brief) | **6.2x fewer gradients per draw at seed 7**, via a larger adapted step size — at 20 divergences against the control's 1. One paired seed; the seed-8 control is running. Posterior agreement is Finding 10 |

## Caveats

**Caveat 1 — every wall clock here is contended, and this project already knows
what that costs.** Three other agents (an SMC campaign, a BlackJAX sampler
survey and a VI evaluation) held this 24-thread box at **load average 18-49**
for the whole session; a `bench/results/.load_trace.log` sampler recorded it at
30-second intervals alongside the runs. The scale of the effect is not
hypothetical and is not small: the same NUTS cell on the same fixture has been
measured at **2450.7 s with five sibling fits on 24 cores and 257.5 s running
clean — a 9.5x spread from scheduling alone**, with R-hat, ESS and divergences
unaffected.

One pair in Finding 1 shows it directly: the same NUTS warmup program,
**byte-identical at 7 883 StableHLO lines**, compiled in 3.65 s at one point in
the session and 6.75 s at another. An identical HLO module cannot cost different
amounts of XLA work; the difference is the machine.

Three consequences, applied throughout:

* **Structural quantities carry no such caveat and are reported as-is** —
  StableHLO line counts and their byte-identity, R-hat, min ESS, divergence
  counts, gradients per effective sample, and the cache entry statistics.
  Findings 1, 3 and 5 rest entirely on these.
* **Ratios taken *within one process* are preferred to comparisons across
  separately-launched runs**, because contention scales all four numbers in one
  process together. Finding 2's decomposition is deliberately of that shape.
* **Any wall clock quoted below is provisional** and is labelled with the load
  it was taken under. A number re-quoted without that label is exactly how the
  142.6 s figure this report exists to correct became a headline.

**Caveat 2 — CPU, one galaxy, D = 8.** These are single-galaxy notebook
fixtures on the CPU, chosen because that is where the published NUTS numbers
live and where the CPU is 13-33x ahead of the 3060 for one galaxy. Nothing here
re-measures the catalog path's wall clock; Findings 3 and 7 are the only ones
that touch it and both are structural rather than timing claims.

**Caveat 3 — SNR 20 under quadrature.** Repeated from the header. A number
measured at SNR 20 under `band_integration="quadrature"` does not transfer to
SNR 300 and does not transfer to the `"taylor"` scheme at any SNR (#1671).

**Caveat 4 — the decomposition in Finding 2 assumes two galaxies cost the same
to sample.** `compile ~= t1 - t3` compares a cold fit on galaxy A against a
warm-but-unadapted fit on galaxy B. They are different draws from the same
generative model, so their sampling costs differ; the `t2` / `t2_b` pair prices
that residual directly and it is reported rather than assumed away.

## What was NOT measured, and why

* **A shared-adaptation catalog engine.** Finding 7 measures what per-galaxy
  adaptation *discovers*; it does not measure a catalog fit that shares one.
  Building that means threading `(step_size, inverse_mass_matrix)` into
  `build_catalog_mcmc_engine`'s `run_one` as traced arguments and hoisting
  `_nuts_warmup_only` out of the `lax.map` — perhaps 60 lines plus a
  `_run_native_mcmc` flag and a contract test — and then a full N = 64 sweep to
  price it. It was not done because Finding 7's spread decides whether it is
  worth doing, and because #1529 already measured the sibling cache doing the
  harmful version of this on NUTS.
* **A GPU re-measurement of anything here.** Single-galaxy CPU is 13-33x ahead
  on this hardware, so the fixtures were run where they are fastest. Whether the
  compile *anatomy* has the same shape on CUDA is unmeasured; XLA's CPU and GPU
  backends are different compilers and the ratios could move.
* **Whether the metric shrinks NUTS's trees.** `2026-08-31_catalog_preconditioning.md`
  named this as "the best next question" and it still is. Finding 6 reports
  R-hat, ESS and wall for preconditioned NUTS but the `tree_depth_*` diagnostics
  were not swept against `precondition`, which is the mechanism by which
  whitening *should* help NUTS most.
* **Catalog NUTS wall clocks.** Phase 3 measured NUTS at 7-15x HMC for the same
  iteration count on the batched path, and a NUTS arm of that sweep would have
  cost more than everything in this report together.
* **FSM-NUTS itself.** Scoped and declined; see that section for the condition
  that would reverse the decision.
* **Whether `prewarm` can be made to compile the fit's shapes without running
  them.** That is a genuine AOT path — `lower(...).compile()` at the caller's
  budget, writing into the persistent cache without executing the scan — and it
  is the fix Finding 4 points at. It is not built here: it means reproducing
  `run_nuts`'s MAP init and preconditioning setup outside `run_nuts` to get the
  shapes right, and doing that by copying is how the two paths drift apart.

## Reproduce

Run from the repository root with `.venv/bin/python`.

```bash
# Finding 1 - the compile anatomy, and the tree-cap sweep that tests the
#   "ragged while loop" story. TENGRI_DISABLE_JAX_CACHE=1 so "compile" is a
#   compile. --skip-run because the compile question does not need the warm
#   call and a 600-step NUTS warmup at cap 10 is minutes of it.
JAX_PLATFORMS=cpu TENGRI_DISABLE_JAX_CACHE=1 \
python bench/scripts/benchmark_nuts_compile.py \
    --notebook 05 --max-doublings 2 6 10 --skip-run \
    --warmup 600 --samples 600 \
    --json bench/results/2026-08-31_nuts_compile_anatomy.json
# ... and again with --notebook ctl-dpl into the same JSON.

# Finding 2 - the decomposition. Four fits in one process: galaxy A twice
#   (the second hits the adaptation cache and is sampling only), then galaxy B
#   twice (the first pays a real warmup, the second does not). Cache ON, because
#   this measures what a user's second fit actually costs.
JAX_PLATFORMS=cpu \
python bench/scripts/benchmark_nuts_compile.py \
    --notebook ctl-jwst --fit-decompose \
    --json bench/results/2026-08-31_nuts_fit_decompose.json

# Finding 4 - the shapes prewarm compiles against the shapes a fit asks for.
#   prewarm runs n_samples=10 at the default n_burnin=100 and n_warmup=300, so
#   these are its two programs; compare their compile column with the 600/600
#   rows from Finding 1.
JAX_PLATFORMS=cpu TENGRI_DISABLE_JAX_CACHE=1 \
python bench/scripts/benchmark_nuts_compile.py \
    --notebook 05 --stage nuts_warmup nuts_scan --skip-run \
    --max-doublings 10 --warmup 300 --samples 110 \
    --json bench/results/2026-08-31_nuts_prewarm_shapes.json

# Finding 5 - the cache. No fit, no GPU: it reads the directory.
python -c "import json; from tengri.utils.jax_cache import cache_stats; \
           print(json.dumps(cache_stats(), indent=1))"

# Findings 6 and 9 - warmup length, and warmup depth, against the analytic
#   metric. One fit per subprocess, seeds from the fixture's own seed upward.
#
#   READ THE JSONL, NOT THE STDOUT LOG, TO TELL HOW FAR A SWEEP HAS GOT.
#   `_append_json` writes one line per completed cell and flushes on close, so
#   the JSONL's line count is an exact count of finished cells. The stdout log
#   is not: redirected to a file it is block-buffered, and the whole 8-row table
#   is far under one 4 KB buffer, so an EMPTY log is equally consistent with
#   "nothing has finished" and with "several cells finished and are sitting in
#   the buffer". During this campaign the control invocation's log was 0 bytes
#   for 47 minutes; what established that it had genuinely completed zero cells
#   was that its --json file did not exist at all.
JAX_PLATFORMS=cpu \
python bench/scripts/benchmark_notebook_sampler.py \
    --notebook ctl-dpl --methods nutswarm --seeds 2 \
    --json bench/results/2026-08-31_nuts_warmup_sweep.jsonl

# Finding 9 - warmup DEPTH rather than warmup length. Sampling stays at cap 10;
#   only the adaptation is capped shallower. Its control is the `nuts w=600`
#   row above, which is the same fit with the two caps equal.
JAX_PLATFORMS=cpu \
python bench/scripts/benchmark_notebook_sampler.py \
    --notebook ctl-dpl --methods nutscap --seeds 2 \
    --json bench/results/2026-08-31_nuts_warmup_cap.jsonl

# TWO PROCESS NOTES, both of which cost time in this campaign.
#
# 1. `pgrep -f "wcap=5+precond"` reports the job MISSING while it is healthy:
#    `+` is a regex quantifier, so the pattern matches "wcap=5precond" and
#    "wcap=55precond" but never the literal label. Use `pgrep -Ff` (fixed
#    string) or escape it. Every config label here containing `+` has this
#    problem.
#
# 2. `ps` makes ONE sweep look like TWO owners of a cell. `_sweep` runs one fit
#    per subprocess, so a `--seeds 2` parent is always listed beside a child of
#    the form `--only <label> --seed <n> --emit-json` for the cell it is
#    currently on. That is a parent and its own child, not two invocations
#    racing. Check `ps -eo pid,ppid` before concluding a cell is being computed
#    twice: killing on the wrong diagnosis destroys work in progress. In this
#    campaign the two suspicious pairs resolved to ppid 32292 and ppid 63197,
#    each the sweep that spawned them.
#
# Split a long --only list across separate invocations rather than one long one:
# a cell that takes 45 minutes blocks every cell behind it, and the harness
# schedules labels in order. Give each invocation its own --json path so two
# cannot append to one file.

# Finding 7 - the spread of what per-galaxy adaptation discovers, on the
#   catalog fixture. Needs no sampling: it stops after warmup.
JAX_DEFAULT_MATMUL_PRECISION=highest \
python bench/scripts/benchmark_catalog_compile.py --adapt-spread 64 \
    --warmup 100 --max-doublings 10 \
    --json bench/results/2026-08-31_catalog_adapt_spread.json

# The gates this branch was held to (the real CI lists, extracted from
# .github/workflows/tests.yml - `lint` and `smoke`), plus the tests that pin
# the two code changes.
.venv/bin/pytest tests/contract/test_batch_mcmc_adaptation_memo.py \
    tests/contract/test_jax_cache.py -q
```

## Addendum — AOT and `jax.export`, scoped without spending the budget

Two claims were put to this report late: that `.lower(...).compile()` and
`jax.export` appear nowhere in `src/tengri/`, and that shape-polymorphic export
might let one artifact serve several band counts. Both halves check out —
`grep` finds no `.lower(` used for AOT and no `jax.export` anywhere in `src/` —
and one structural fact decides how much that is worth.

**The big arrays are already traced, not baked.** `Fitter._build_data_args`
threads `ssp_data` and `template_data` into a `_jit_inputs` sub-dict
specifically so the loss sees them as *outer Parameters, not Constants*, and its
comment records what happened when `ForwardModel` failed to delegate
`ssp_data`: "every fit through it baked the SSP grid into the compiled program
as a constant and XLA was OOM-killed on large grids" (#1496). So:

* the usual motivation for AOT — pulling large constants out of the artifact —
  **does not apply, because it was already done**, by a different mechanism and
  for a different reason;
* a compiled program already serves any galaxy, and any SSP grid of the same
  shape, without recompiling;
* the 100+ MB cache entries of Finding 5 are therefore **program**, not payload.

That leaves shape polymorphism as the only genuinely new thing `jax.export`
would buy: one artifact across varying `n_bands` / `n_wave`. The `n_gal` axis is
already handled without it — `_resolve_n_padded` pads the catalog so different
catalog sizes share one compile, and Phase 3 verified StableHLO byte-identical
across a 16x sweep in N.

**This was not attempted, and Finding 1 is why.** A cold single-galaxy NUTS fit
contains about 13 s of XLA. Shape-polymorphic export is a substantial piece of
work — every `reshape` to a concrete size and every Python-level branch on a
shape in the forward path is a potential blocker, and finding them means
attempting the export rather than grepping for them — and the prize is a share
of 13 s, paid once per band count, on a path where the persistent cache already
removes it across processes. It is worth doing only if compile turns out to be
large, which is the question Finding 2 answers. The order matters: measure
first, and the measurement is cheap while the export is not.
